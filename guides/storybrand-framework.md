# StoryBrand Framework (after Donald Miller)

**Attribution:** This guide summarizes Donald Miller's StoryBrand framework from *Building a StoryBrand* (HarperCollins Leadership, 2017; revised as *Building a StoryBrand 2.0*, 2024). It is a concept-level summary in our own words so an agent can apply the structure to websites, proposals, and emails. **Buy the book** — Miller's full treatment, examples, and the BrandScript exercise are worth the price many times over.

Use this structure as the default narrative skeleton for all marketing output: websites, landing pages, proposals, email sequences, and social copy.

---

## The Core Principle

**The customer is the hero. The business is the guide.** Every failed marketing page makes the same mistake: it casts the company as the protagonist. In Miller's framing, the business plays the mentor role — the experienced party that helps the hero win. Position the customer at the center of the story and the business in service of them, always.

## The 7-Part Narrative Arc

Miller structures marketing as a story in seven movements:

### 1. A Character (the customer)
Open with who the customer is and what they want. Use their language, not industry jargon. The stated want must be simple and specific — one desire, clearly named.

### 2. Has a Problem
Define the villain: the root cause of the customer's frustration. Miller distinguishes three levels, and the middle one is the most powerful:

- **External** — the tangible surface issue (an outdated website, no online presence).
- **Internal** — how it makes them *feel* (embarrassed, overwhelmed, behind their competitors). Always include this level; people buy solutions to internal problems.
- **Philosophical** — why the situation is simply wrong ("people who do great work shouldn't lose customers to a worse competitor with a better website").

Lead with the problem before ever presenting the solution.

### 3. And Meets a Guide
Only now does the business enter — as the guide, established through two signals:

- **Empathy:** show you understand their situation.
- **Authority:** brief, concrete proof you can help — results, credentials, volume of work. One or two proof points, not a resume. Never claim "we're the best"; demonstrate it.

### 4. Who Gives Them a Plan
A simple three-step plan that removes confusion and makes the next step feel easy and safe: "1. Book a call → 2. We build your system → 3. Watch it work." The plan's job is to reduce the perceived risk of acting. If the steps sound complicated, simplify until they don't.

### 5. And Calls Them to Action
Every page and every long section needs a clear call to action.

- **Direct CTA:** the primary action (Book a Call, Get Started) — a visually dominant button, repeated at least in the header, mid-page, and footer.
- **Transitional CTA:** a lower-commitment option for people not ready (download a guide, watch a demo).

Never end a page or email without a CTA.

### 6. That Helps Them Avoid Failure
Name what's at stake if they don't act — honestly, without fear-mongering. "Without a credible online presence, you're invisible to the customers searching right now" creates urgency while staying true.

### 7. And Ends in Success
Close with a specific picture of life after working with you — not "your business will grow," but a concrete outcome ("a site that produces double-digit qualified leads a month while you run the crew"). Use transformation language: from the painful current state to the desired future state. This is the last emotion the reader should feel.

## Applying the Arc to Deliverables

### Websites
- **Hero section:** a one-liner that passes Miller's "grunt test" — within five seconds anyone can tell what you offer, how it improves their life, and what to do next.
- **Stakes section:** the cost of not acting.
- **Value/success section:** the transformation.
- **Plan section:** the three steps.
- **Proof:** testimonials — other heroes who won with this guide.
- **CTA:** repeated header, mid-page, footer.
- **Junk-drawer test:** any section that doesn't serve the narrative gets cut.

### Cold Emails
Subject hints at the hero's problem; opening line names their specific pain (external and internal); one sentence of empathy plus authority; one CTA, not three. Keep the whole email short — under roughly 75 words.

### Proposals
Order the document as story, not as brochure: their problem first, then the cost of not solving it, then the plan, then the success picture, then investment and next step. Never open a proposal with the agency's bio.

### Social Posts
Hook calls out the hero and their problem; body delivers empathy plus insight; close with a CTA or a success vision.

## The One-Liner Formula

Every business needs a StoryBrand one-liner: **[Problem] + [Solution] + [Result].**

Template: "Most [audience] lose customers because [problem]. We [solution] — so you can [result]."

## Standing Rules for the Agent

- Never open a website, email, or proposal with the business. Open with the customer's problem.
- Never use hero language for the business ("we are the leading...", "our innovative solutions..."). Guide language only.
- Always include a CTA; no page or email ends without one.
- Always address the internal problem (the feeling), not just the external one.
- When writing for a client, translate their business into the frame: the client is the guide, their customer is the hero.
- Apply the grunt test to every homepage header before shipping.
- Keep every three-step plan genuinely simple.
